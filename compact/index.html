module 6.1 · dimensionality reduction
Principal Component Analysis
Every variant in our run is a point in four-dimensional space. Here is one of ours, chr20:32,793,423, exactly as the pipeline wrote it down:
QD
34.89
FS
0.000
MQ
60.00
SOR
5.892
Four measurements means four axes. Paper has two. To draw all 1,775 at once, two dimensions have to go — and the entire question is which two.
Think of a shadow. Hold an object under a lamp and turn it: from one angle the shadow shows the whole outline, from another it collapses into a blur. PCA turns the data until the shadow is the most informative one available, keeps the shadow, and throws the object away.
what the algorithm actually does
01Standardise. Put all four features on one scale, so none wins just by being measured in bigger numbers.
02Covariance. Build the 4×4 table of how strongly each pair of features moves together.
03Eigenvectors. That table hands back four directions, all at right angles, each with a number saying how much spread it holds.
04Rank and keep. Sort those directions by that number and keep the top two. Everything below is thrown away.
05Project. Give every variant its two new coordinates, PC1 and PC2.
No training, no labels, no guessing. Spread is information, and those five steps are simply how a machine finds the angle that keeps the most of it.

module 3.2 · ensemble learning
Bagging & the Random Forest
Grow a decision tree on our 1,775 variants and it does a passable job. Grow it again on a slightly different sample and you get a different tree, making different mistakes. Here are the first three we built — same algorithm, same data, and this is the share of flagged variants each one caught on the rows it was never shown:
TREE 1
0.867
TREE 2
1.000
TREE 3
0.857
A single tree is not a stable thing to own. Bagging — bootstrap aggregating — does not try to fix that. It farms it: build two hundred unstable trees, each on its own random resample, and let them vote.
Ask one doctor, or ask two hundred. Any one of them can be having a bad day. Their errors point in every direction, so they cancel; the thing they agree on is usually the diagnosis. You are not making anyone smarter — you are cancelling mistakes against each other.
what the algorithm actually does
01Bootstrap. Draw 1,775 rows at random with replacement. Some arrive twice, some not at all, so this tree gets its own private version of the data.
02Grow a tree. Split the sample on whichever number best separates flagged from kept, then split again, all the way down.
03Hide most features. At every split the tree may consider only a random handful of the columns — which stops all two hundred from copying each other.
04Repeat. Two hundred times. Nothing passes between trees; none of them ever sees another’s answer.
05Vote. Every tree gets one ballot per variant. The majority wins.
The rows a tree was not given are its out-of-bag set — a free, honest exam paper that it has never seen. Every score on this slide is measured that way, so nothing here is a model grading its own homework.

module 2.3 · performance metrics
The Confusion Matrix & Its Children
Last slide’s forest got 1,766 of 1,775 calls right. That is 99.5% accurate. Here is the same score for a “model” that is one line long — return KEEP — and catches not a single bad variant:
THE FOREST
99.5%
ONE LINE
98.2%
GAP
1.3
Thirteen-tenths of one percent is all that separates a working filter from a function that does nothing. Accuracy is not a lie here — it is just answering a question nobody asked. The rest of this slide is the vocabulary for asking better ones.
A smoke alarm that never goes off is right on almost every day of your life. You would still not put one in your kitchen. What you actually want to know is: when there is a fire, does it ring — and when it rings, is there a fire?
the five things you are expected to define
01Confusion matrix. A 2×2 table: what was true across the top, what the model said down the side. Four cells — TP, FP, FN, TN. Every other number on this slide is arithmetic on these four.
02Precision. TP / (TP + FP). Of everything you flagged, how much deserved it. The cost of getting it wrong is a good variant in the bin.
03Recall (sensitivity). TP / (TP + FN). Of everything that deserved flagging, how much you caught. The cost of getting it wrong is a bad variant in the report.
04F-measure. The harmonic mean of the two. Unlike the ordinary average, it refuses to be flattered by one high number and one terrible one.
05ROC. Stop picking one cut-off. Sweep every cut-off, plot what you catch against what you break, and read the area underneath.
Only 1.8% of our rows are flagged. Whenever one class is that rare, accuracy is dominated by the class you were not asking about — which is why every number here is built from TP and ignores TN entirely.

The Same Decision, Made Three Ways
Every filter in this talk answers one question about one row: is this variant real? Nobody has changed the question in fifteen years. What keeps changing is what you are allowed to look at before you answer:
GEN 1
4 numbers
GEN 2
4 numbers
GEN 3
the reads
Generations one and two argue about where to draw the line through those four numbers. Generation three throws the four numbers away and looks at the evidence they were summarising.
A doctor, a doctor with a chart, and a doctor who wants the X-ray. The first two are reading the same summary and disagreeing about the cut-off. The third has stopped trusting the summary.
what each generation actually does
01Hard filtering. A human writes the thresholds into a config file — SOR > 3.0, QD < 2.0, MQ < 40. This is the pipeline we ran. It is fast, it is auditable, and it cannot adapt to your data.
02Learn the boundary. GATK’s VQSR fits a Gaussian mixture to the same four numbers, using known variant sites as the training signal, and scores every row by how well it fits the “real variant” cluster. Our random forest did the same job by a different route — and rediscovered the humans’ cut-off.
03Skip the summary. DeepVariant does not read QD or SOR at all. It renders the stacked reads at a locus as an image and hands it to a convolutional network — the same architecture family that classifies photographs.
